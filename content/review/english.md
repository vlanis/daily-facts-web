- [~] **1.** "I'm good" and "I'm well" are not interchangeable. "How are you?" — "I'm good" describes your general state; "I'm well" specifically means you are not ill. Native speakers use "good" far more often, so the textbook correction to "well" is usually the less natural choice.
  <br>↳ *Merriam-Webster, usage notes on good/well*

- [~] **2.** English has no future tense — not grammatically. "Will" is a modal verb, and "going to", the present continuous and even the present simple all express future time. That is why "My train leaves at six" is perfectly correct for tomorrow.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*

- [~] **3.** Adjective order in English is fixed, and every native speaker follows it without ever being taught it: opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose. "A lovely little old rectangular green French silver whittling knife" sounds fine; reorder any two words and it sounds broken.
  <br>↳ *Mark Forsyth, «The Elements of Eloquence»*

- [~] **4.** "Literally" has been used to mean "figuratively" for over two centuries — Dickens, Joyce and Austen all did it. Dictionaries now record both senses, which infuriates purists but simply reflects how the word has always actually been used.
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary, entry for «literally»*

- [~] **5.** Use "fewer" for things you can count and "less" for things you cannot: fewer bottles, less water. The supermarket sign "10 items or less" is the most-complained-about grammar mistake in Britain — several chains have quietly changed theirs to "fewer".
  <br>↳ *Plain English Campaign; Tesco signage change (2008)*

- [~] **6.** A useful pair to keep straight: "I could care less" literally means you do care somewhat. The intended phrase is "I couldn't care less" — you care so little that less is impossible.
  <br>↳ *Merriam-Webster, usage notes*

- [~] **7.** The word "set" has one of the longest entries in the Oxford English Dictionary — over 400 distinct senses. "Run" has since overtaken it, with more than 600 meanings in modern use.
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary; Simon Winchester on the OED*

- [~] **8.** English borrowed so heavily that roughly 60% of its vocabulary comes from Latin and French. The everyday words tend to be Germanic and the formal ones Romance — which is why you "ask" a friend but "enquire" at a desk, and eat "pork" (French) from a "pig" (English).
  <br>↳ *David Crystal, «The Stories of English»*

- [~] **9.** "Nice" originally meant "foolish" or "ignorant", from the Latin «nescius». Over 700 years it drifted through "fussy", "precise" and "delicate" before settling on "pleasant" — one of the most complete meaning reversals in the language.
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary, etymology of «nice»*

- [~] **10.** A phrasal verb changes meaning completely depending on the particle: to "take off" (leave the ground), "take in" (deceive or absorb), "take on" (accept a challenge), "take over" (assume control). This is the single hardest part of English for learners, because none of it can be deduced from «take».
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, phrasal verbs with «take»*

- [~] **11.** "Whom" is dying, and that is fine. It survives mainly after prepositions — "to whom it may concern", "with whom I spoke". In ordinary speech, "who did you give it to?" is now standard, and insisting on "whom" can sound stiff rather than educated.
  <br>↳ *Merriam-Webster, usage notes on who/whom*

- [~] **12.** The most useful hedging phrases in professional English are not grammar but tone: "I'd say", "it seems that", "as far as I know", "correct me if I'm wrong". They let you make a claim while leaving room to be wrong — which native speakers do constantly.
  <br>↳ *Academic and business English usage guides*

- [~] **13.** "Actually" in English does not mean "currently" — that is a false friend with many European languages. "Actually" means "in fact" and often signals a polite correction: "Actually, the meeting is at three."
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «actually»*

- [~] **14.** The letter combination "ough" can be pronounced at least eight different ways: though, through, thought, tough, cough, bough, thorough, hiccough. This is a fossil of pronunciation shifts that spelling never caught up with.
  <br>↳ *David Crystal, «Spell It Out»*

- [~] **15.** English lost its formal "you" the other way around from most languages. "Thou" was the informal singular; "you" was the polite plural. Politeness won so completely that the intimate form disappeared entirely, leaving English the only major European language with no tu/vous distinction.
  <br>↳ *David Crystal, «The Stories of English»*

- [~] **16.** Two useful softeners for disagreement: "I see what you mean, but…" and "That's fair, though…". Both signal you have understood before objecting, which in English matters more than the objection itself.
  <br>↳ *Business English usage guides*

- [~] **17.** "Sorry" in British English is rarely an apology. It works as "excuse me" (getting past someone), "pardon?" (I didn't hear), and even as mild protest ("sorry, but that's not right"). Counting apologies in a British conversation badly overstates the contrition.
  <br>↳ *British English pragmatics*

- [~] **18.** The present perfect trips up most learners because it links past to present: "I have lost my keys" means they are still lost. "I lost my keys" is a finished event — you may well have found them since.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*

- [~] **19.** "Used to" and "be used to" are unrelated. "I used to smoke" is a past habit now stopped. "I'm used to smoking" means it feels normal. The second takes -ing, the first a bare verb.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, usage notes*

- [~] **20.** English has no word for the day after tomorrow, though many languages do. Speakers manage with the phrase itself, or borrow *overmorrow*, an archaic word that never quite came back.
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary, «overmorrow»*

- [~] **21.** Stress changes the part of speech in dozens of English pairs: *REcord* the noun versus *reCORD* the verb, *PREsent* versus *preSENT*, *OBject* versus *obJECT*. The spelling never changes, so only listening reveals it.
  <br>↳ *English phonology: initial-stress-derived nouns*

- [~] **22.** "Do you mind…?" is answered backwards. "Do you mind if I sit here?" — "No, not at all" means yes, please do. Answering "yes" means you object, which is why the polite reply is usually a phrase rather than a bare word.
  <br>↳ *English pragmatics; Cambridge Dictionary*

- [~] **23.** Contractions are not casual sloppiness — they are the norm. Writing "I do not think so" instead of "I don't think so" sounds emphatic or cold, not more correct. Full forms are for stress and formal documents.
  <br>↳ *English usage guides*

- [~] **24.** The phrase "with all due respect" almost always precedes disrespect. Native speakers hear it as a warning, not a courtesy — much like "no offence, but".
  <br>↳ *English pragmatics*

- [~] **25.** English is unusual in placing adjectives before the noun and keeping them there. "The red big car" is wrong, "the big red car" is right, and no native speaker can explain why without being told the rule exists.
  <br>↳ *Mark Forsyth, «The Elements of Eloquence»*

- [~] **26.** "Shall" survives mainly in questions offering to do something: "Shall I open the window?" As a future marker it has almost vanished from speech, though legal English still uses it to impose obligations.
  <br>↳ *Merriam-Webster; legal drafting conventions*

- [~] **27.** "Make" versus "do" has a rough rule: "do" is for activities and duties (do homework, do the dishes, do business), "make" is for creating or producing something (make a cake, make a mistake, make a decision). The exceptions just have to be memorised — "make the bed" is one.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Grammar of English*

- [~] **28.** "Eventually" does not mean "possibly" — another false friend for speakers of many European languages. "Eventually" means "in the end, after a long time": "We eventually found the hotel." For "possibly", say "possibly", "perhaps" or "potentially".
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary*

- [~] **29.** "I'm afraid" is the politest way to deliver bad news in English, and it has nothing to do with fear: "I'm afraid the meeting's been cancelled." It softens the message without apologising for it.
  <br>↳ *Usage; Oxford Learner's Dictionaries*

- [~] **30.** "Sympathetic" in English means "showing compassion", not "pleasant" or "likeable" as in many European languages. A likeable person is "nice", "pleasant" or "easy to get along with".
  <br>↳ *False friends; Cambridge Dictionary*

- [~] **31.** "Since" and "for" with the present perfect: "since" takes a starting point (since 2019, since Monday), "for" takes a length of time (for five years, for a week). "I've lived here since five years" is one of the most common learner errors.
  <br>↳ *English Grammar in Use, Murphy*

- [~] **32.** The word "OK" began as a joke. In 1839 Boston newspapers had a fad for comic abbreviations of misspelled phrases, and "O.K." stood for "oll korrect". Almost all the others died; this one went global.
  <br>↳ *Allan Metcalf, «OK: The Improbable Story of America's Greatest Word»*

- [~] **33.** "Hello" is surprisingly young as a greeting. It became common only with the telephone in the late 19th century — Thomas Edison pushed it as the word to answer with; Alexander Graham Bell preferred "ahoy".
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary; Smithsonian*

- [~] **34.** Queuing vocabulary: in British English you "queue" or "stand in a queue"; in American English you "stand in line" or "wait in line". "Jump the queue" (UK) and "cut in line" (US) mean pushing in ahead of others.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary*

- [~] **35.** British "flat", American "apartment"; British "lift", American "elevator"; British "biscuit", American "cookie". But a British "biscuit" is closer to the American "cookie", while an American "biscuit" is a soft savoury scone.
  <br>↳ *Oxford Learner's Dictionaries*

- [~] **36.** "Pants" is a classic trap. In American English it means trousers; in British English it means underwear. Saying "I spilled coffee on my pants" in London will get a different reaction from the one you expect.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary*

- [~] **37.** Question tags reverse the polarity of the sentence: "You're coming, aren't you?", "She doesn't smoke, does she?". The tone matters too — falling intonation means "I'm checking what I already think", rising means a real question.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Grammar of English*

- [~] **38.** "Fun" versus "funny": something "fun" is enjoyable; something "funny" makes you laugh — or is strange. "The party was fun" is a compliment; "the party was funny" may mean something odd happened.
  <br>↳ *Usage*

- [~] **39.** The second conditional is for unreal or unlikely situations: "If I won the lottery, I would travel." Note "if I were" is standard for all persons in careful English — "If I were you…" — though "if I was" is common in speech.
  <br>↳ *English Grammar in Use, Murphy*

- [~] **40.** "I wish" is followed by a past form even when you mean the present: "I wish I had more time" (I don't now), "I wish it wasn't raining". For regrets about the past, step back once more: "I wish I had studied harder."
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Grammar of English*

- [~] **41.** "Shakespeare added around 1,700 words to English" is overstated — many were simply first recorded in his works. But he certainly popularised dozens of phrases still in use: "break the ice", "wild-goose chase", "in a pickle", "heart of gold".
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary; Folger Shakespeare Library*

- [~] **42.** "Its" and "it's" are different words. "It's" is always "it is" or "it has"; "its" is the possessive, with no apostrophe: "The company changed its name." If you can expand it to "it is", you need the apostrophe.
  <br>↳ *Usage*

- [~] **43.** "Affect" is usually the verb and "effect" usually the noun: the weather affects my mood; the weather has an effect on my mood. "To effect change" is a rarer verb meaning "to bring about".
  <br>↳ *Merriam-Webster*

- [~] **44.** "Loose" (not tight) and "lose" (misplace, fail to win) differ by one letter and are among the most frequent spelling errors even among native speakers. The pronunciation helps: "loose" ends in an /s/, "lose" in a /z/.
  <br>↳ *Usage*

- [~] **45.** "Bring" and "take" depend on direction: "bring" is towards the speaker or listener, "take" is away. "Bring your laptop to the meeting" (where I'll be); "Take an umbrella when you go out".
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary*

- [~] **46.** The word "goodbye" is a contraction of "God be with ye", shortened over the 16th–17th centuries. "Good" replaced "God" probably by analogy with "good day" and "good night".
  <br>↳ *Online Etymology Dictionary*

- [~] **47.** "Robot" entered English from Czech. Karel Čapek's 1920 play «R.U.R.» introduced it, from «robota», meaning forced labour; his brother Josef suggested the word.
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary*

- [~] **48.** English has borrowed a handful of words through Ukrainian, the best known being "Cossack" (via Ukrainian «козак» and Polish, ultimately from Turkic) and "hetman". "Borscht", by contrast, reached English mainly through Yiddish.
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary*

- [~] **49.** The silent "b" in "debt" and "doubt" was added on purpose. Middle English had "dette" and "doute"; Renaissance scholars inserted the "b" to show the Latin roots «debitum» and «dubitare». Nobody ever pronounced it.
  <br>↳ *David Crystal, «Spell It Out»*

- [~] **50.** "Island" never had an "s" sound. The Old English word was «iegland»; the "s" was added in the 1500s by analogy with the unrelated French-derived "isle".
  <br>↳ *Online Etymology Dictionary*

- [~] **51.** The pronoun "they" for one person of unknown gender is not new — it has been used since the 14th century, by Chaucer and Shakespeare among others. In 2019 Merriam-Webster named singular "they" its Word of the Year.
  <br>↳ *Merriam-Webster*

- [~] **52.** "Fortnight" — two weeks — is everyday British English but almost unknown in American speech. It comes from Old English for "fourteen nights".
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary*

- [~] **53.** A polite way to interrupt in a meeting: "Sorry to jump in, but…" or "Can I just come in here?" To get the floor back after being interrupted: "If I could just finish my point…".
  <br>↳ *Business English usage*

- [~] **54.** "Would you mind…?" is followed by the -ing form: "Would you mind closing the window?" "Would you mind if I…" uses the past: "Would you mind if I opened the window?"
  <br>↳ *English Grammar in Use, Murphy*

- [~] **55.** "Make sense" and "make sense of" are different. "It makes sense" means it's logical; "I can't make sense of it" means I can't understand it. "Does that make sense?" is a standard check after explaining something.
  <br>↳ *Usage*

- [~] **56.** "Look forward to" ends with a preposition, so it takes -ing: "I look forward to hearing from you", not "to hear from you". It's the standard formal closing line of an email.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary*

- [~] **57.** "Interested" versus "interesting": you are interested in a book; the book is interesting. Saying "I am interesting in history" is a common slip — and makes it sound as though you are fascinating.
  <br>↳ *Usage; -ed/-ing adjectives*

- [~] **58.** English stresses content words and squeezes the rest. In "I want to GO to the SHOP", "to" and "the" shrink to a neutral "schwa" sound. That is why native speech can sound like a stream of important words with mush in between.
  <br>↳ *Phonetics; Roach, «English Phonetics and Phonology»*

- [~] **59.** The schwa — the vowel sound in "a-bout" and "sof-a" — is the most common vowel in English, and it can be spelled with any vowel letter: about, taken, pencil, memory, supply.
  <br>↳ *Phonetics*

- [~] **60.** "Actually" in British conversation often signals a gentle correction: "Actually, I think it's on Thursday." Using it too often can sound like contradicting everyone.
  <br>↳ *Usage*

- [~] **61.** "Fancy" as a verb is very British: "Do you fancy a coffee?" means "Would you like one?", and "I think she fancies you" means she's attracted to you. Americans mostly use it as an adjective — a fancy restaurant.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary*

- [~] **62.** Understatement is a British cultural code. "Not bad" can be high praise; "that's a bit annoying" may mean a disaster; "I hear what you say" often means "I disagree and won't discuss it further".
  <br>↳ *Kate Fox, «Watching the English»*

- [~] **63.** "Gonna", "wanna" and "gotta" are fine in speech and informal messages, but not in formal writing. Note "wanna" only replaces "want to" — "I want a coffee" can't become "I wanna coffee" in careful English.
  <br>↳ *Usage*

- [~] **64.** Collocations are why English sounds "right" or "wrong" even when the grammar is fine. You "make a mistake" (not do), "take a photo" (not make), "pay attention" (not give), "heavy rain" (not strong rain).
  <br>↳ *Oxford Collocations Dictionary*

- [~] **65.** "Strong tea" but "heavy rain"; "high temperature" but "great importance"; "deeply sorry" but "highly unlikely". Learning words in pairs like these is faster than learning them alone.
  <br>↳ *Oxford Collocations Dictionary*

- [~] **66.** English spelling froze before pronunciation finished changing. The Great Vowel Shift (roughly 1400–1700) changed how long vowels sounded, but printing had already fixed the spelling — which is why "bite", "meat" and "name" are spelled the way they are.
  <br>↳ *David Crystal; history of English*

- [~] **67.** "Knight" was once pronounced with every letter, including the "k" and a throaty sound for "gh" — roughly "k-nicht". The spelling stayed while the sounds wore away.
  <br>↳ *History of English phonology*

- [~] **68.** The longest word in major English dictionaries without repeating a letter is often given as "uncopyrightable" — fifteen letters, each used once.
  <br>↳ *Word records*

- [~] **69.** Some nouns are uncountable in English even when they are countable in Ukrainian: "advice", "information", "furniture", "luggage", "news". There is no "an advice" or "informations" — say "a piece of advice", "some information".
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Grammar of English*

- [~] **70.** "Remember" and "remind" are easy to swap. You remember something yourself; someone or something reminds you: "Remind me to call her" — not "remember me to call her", which means "pass on my greetings".
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary*

- [~] **71.** "Wait for" and "await": you wait for something; you await something (no preposition). "I'm waiting for your reply" or "I await your reply" — but never "I'm awaiting for".
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary*

- [~] **72.** "Lay" and "lie" confuse native speakers too. "Lay" needs an object: lay the book on the table. "Lie" doesn't: lie on the sofa. The trap is that the past tense of "lie" is "lay".
  <br>↳ *Merriam-Webster*

- [~] **73.** "Who" versus "that": for people, both are correct ("the man who called", "the man that called"); for things, use "that" or "which". "The company who…" is common but jars for many careful readers.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Grammar of English*

- [~] **74.** "Emoji" is not from "emotion". It's Japanese: «e» (picture) + «moji» (character). The resemblance to "emotion" is pure coincidence.
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary*

- [~] **75.** English has no academy that regulates it, unlike French or Spanish. Dictionaries such as Oxford and Merriam-Webster describe how people actually use the language rather than legislate it.
  <br>↳ *Linguistics; descriptive lexicography*

- [~] **76.** "Ketchup" probably comes from a Hokkien Chinese word for a fermented fish sauce, «kê-tsiap». The tomato version only appeared in America in the 19th century.
  <br>↳ *Dan Jurafsky, «The Language of Food»*

- [~] **77.** "Salary" is often said to come from Roman soldiers being paid in salt. The word does come from Latin «sal» (salt), but historians find no solid evidence that soldiers were literally paid in it.
  <br>↳ *Online Etymology Dictionary*

- [~] **78.** Phrasal verbs often have a formal one-word twin: "find out" — discover, "put off" — postpone, "come up with" — devise, "look into" — investigate. Use the phrasal verb in speech and the single word in reports.
  <br>↳ *Usage; register*

- [~] **79.** "Hopefully" was once condemned by style guides when used to mean "it is hoped that": "Hopefully, the train will be on time." The Associated Press style guide finally accepted it in 2012 — usage had simply won.
  <br>↳ *AP Stylebook, 2012*
