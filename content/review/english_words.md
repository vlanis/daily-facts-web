- [~] **1.** **Set** — probably the most overloaded word in English. (1) A group belonging together: *a set of keys*. (2) To place something down: *set it on the table*. (3) To become firm: *let the jelly set*. (4) Ready: *are you all set?* The dictionary lists hundreds of senses; these four cover most daily use.
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary, entry for «set»*

- [~] **2.** **Fine** — the meaning flips with tone. (1) Of high quality: *fine wine*. (2) Acceptable, only just: *"How was it?" "Fine."* (3) Very thin or small: *fine sand*, *fine print*. (4) A punishment payment: *a parking fine*. Sense 2 said flatly usually means the opposite of sense 1.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «fine»*

- [~] **3.** **Mean** — three unrelated senses share one spelling. (1) To signify: *what does this mean?* (2) Unkind: *don't be mean to her*. (3) Average, in maths: *the mean value*. A fourth, chiefly British: *mean* as stingy — *he's mean with money*.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «mean»*

- [~] **4.** **Run** — the word with the most senses in modern English, over 600 by some counts. (1) Move fast on foot. (2) Operate or manage: *she runs a company*. (3) Function: *the engine runs well*. (4) Flow or leak: *your nose is running*. Only the first is about legs.
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary; Simon Winchester on «run»*

- [~] **5.** **Draw** — (1) To make a picture: *draw a map*. (2) To pull: *draw the curtains*. (3) To attract: *the show drew a crowd*. (4) A tie, no winner: *the match ended in a draw*. Sense 2 is the original; drawing pictures came from pulling a pen across a surface.
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary, «draw»*

- [~] **6.** **Book** — (1) The object you read. (2) To reserve: *book a table*. (3) To formally charge someone: *the police booked him*. Senses 2 and 3 both come from writing a name into a ledger — a book.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «book»*

- [~] **7.** **Light** — two different words that collided. (1) Not heavy: *a light bag*. (2) Illumination: *turn on the light*. (3) To set alight: *light a candle*. (4) Pale: *light blue*. Senses 2-4 come from one Old English root, sense 1 from a completely different one.
  <br>↳ *Oxford English Dictionary, etymology of «light»*

- [~] **8.** **Still** — (1) Not moving: *sit still*. (2) Up to now: *are you still here?* (3) Even so: *it's expensive; still, I want it*. (4) Not fizzy: *still water*, the standard opposite of *sparkling* on a menu.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «still»*

- [~] **9.** **Kind** — (1) Generous, warm: *a kind person*. (2) A type: *what kind of music?* (3) In *kind of*, a softener meaning "somewhat": *it's kind of cold*. Sense 3 is extremely common in speech and almost absent from textbooks.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «kind»*

- [~] **10.** **Charge** — (1) A price: *there's no charge*. (2) To accuse formally: *charged with theft*. (3) To refill a battery. (4) To rush forward: *the bull charged*. Also *in charge of* — responsible for. Four senses that share almost nothing.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «charge»*

- [~] **11.** **Miss** — (1) To fail to hit or catch: *miss the train*. (2) To feel someone's absence: *I miss you*. (3) To not notice: *you missed the point*. Sense 2 is unusual across languages — many use a construction closer to "you are lacking to me".
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «miss»*

- [~] **12.** **Pretty** — (1) Attractive: *a pretty dress*. (2) An intensifier meaning "quite" or "fairly": *pretty good*, *pretty bad*. Sense 2 is far more frequent in speech, and confusingly it can mean either "moderately" or "very" depending on stress.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «pretty»*

- [x] **13.** **Get** — the workhorse of spoken English, and the reason learners sound formal without it. (1) Obtain: *get a coffee*. (2) Understand: *I get it*. (3) Become: *getting cold*. (4) Arrive: *get home*. Native speakers use it constantly; textbooks avoid it.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «get»*

- [x] **14.** **Break** — (1) To damage: *break a glass*. (2) A pause: *take a break*. (3) To fail to keep: *break a promise*. (4) To make public: *the news broke*. Also *break even* — to finish with neither profit nor loss.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «break»*

- [x] **15.** **Point** — (1) A sharp end: *the point of a pencil*. (2) The purpose or main idea: *that's not the point*. (3) A unit of score: *three points*. (4) A moment: *at that point*. The phrase *there's no point* means it is pointless, not that a point is missing.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «point»*

- [x] **16.** **Hold** — (1) To grip: *hold my bag*. (2) To contain: *the room holds fifty*. (3) To remain true: *the rule still holds*. (4) On the phone: *please hold*. Also *hold on* meaning wait, and *hold up* meaning both delay and rob.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «hold»*

- [x] **17.** **Matter** — (1) To be important: *it doesn't matter*. (2) A subject: *a private matter*. (3) Physical substance: *organic matter*. The phrase *what's the matter?* means "what is wrong?", not "what is the subject?".
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «matter»*

- [x] **18.** **Bear** — (1) The animal. (2) To carry: *bear the weight*. (3) To tolerate: *I can't bear it*. (4) To produce: *bear fruit*. The past forms split confusingly: *bore*, *borne* — except *born* for being given birth to.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «bear»*

- [x] **19.** **Fair** — (1) Just: *a fair decision*. (2) Light-coloured: *fair hair*. (3) Moderate, only adequate: *a fair chance*. (4) A funfair or trade fair. And *fair enough* means "I accept that", conceding without fully agreeing.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «fair»*

- [x] **20.** **Stand** — (1) To be upright. (2) To tolerate: *I can't stand him*. (3) To remain valid: *the offer stands*. (4) A booth or a position: *a market stand*, *take a stand*. Sense 2 is far more common in speech than sense 1.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «stand»*

- [x] **21.** **Just** — a small word doing four jobs. (1) Only: *just one*. (2) A moment ago: *he just left*. (3) Exactly: *just right*. (4) Fair: *a just society*. Sense 4 is the original and now the rarest in speech.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «just»*

- [x] **22.** **Order** — (1) A request for goods: *place an order*. (2) A command. (3) Arrangement: *in alphabetical order*. (4) Tidiness or lawfulness: *law and order*. Note *out of order* means both broken and behaving unacceptably.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «order»*

- [x] **23.** **Sound** — (1) Noise. (2) To seem: *that sounds good*. (3) Healthy or reliable: *sound advice*, *safe and sound*. (4) A narrow sea channel. Sense 2 is the one learners underuse — it is the natural way to react to a suggestion.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «sound»*

- [x] **24.** **Charge**, **cover** and **draw** each carry a commercial sense worth knowing: *charge* a price, *cover* a cost, *draw* a salary. All three also have everyday physical meanings, which is why financial English reads oddly until the second sense clicks.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, business English usage*

- [x] **25.** **Rather** — (1) Preference: *I'd rather stay*. (2) An intensifier meaning fairly: *rather good*. (3) A correction: *he's a colleague, or rather a friend*. British English uses sense 2 far more than American.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary, «rather»*

- [x] **26.** **Quite** — the transatlantic trap. In British English *quite good* usually means moderately good, faintly damning; in American English it leans towards very good. Same words, opposite compliment.
  <br>↳ *Cambridge Dictionary; British vs American usage notes*
